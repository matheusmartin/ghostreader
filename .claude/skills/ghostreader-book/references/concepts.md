# Concepts, examples and relations

A reader who comes back to a book needs its ideas and how they fit together: the claims the author
makes, the concepts the claims are made of, the examples that make them clear or believable, and
the relations between all of these. Single terms are easy to keep; the links between them are
what a reader loses first and what a returning reader must rebuild.

Find them as a student does who must explain the chapter tomorrow without the book: by reading for
meaning, not for key words. Record each with its anchor, then show them as diagrams
(`references/visuals.md`, "From relations to diagrams").

## Read twice (step 5)

1. **First read: what is there.** Read the chapter in full. Write its claims, concepts and
   examples (and, for fiction, its scenes and people).
2. **Second read: how it fits.** Read it again with that list beside you. For each passage, decide
   what it does in the chapter (the outline), and record the relations between the items.

Linking words ("because", "but", "for example") help, but they never decide. Most links in a book
have no such word: the author puts two cases side by side, tells a story before its point, or lets
the order of the chapter carry the argument. And one word does many jobs: "but" can mark a
contrast, a limit, an objection or a new topic. Decide each relation from what the passage means
and where it stands.

## What counts

### Claims

What the author says is true: the chapter's thesis and the claims that support it. Authors often
put them in the title and the subheadings, the first and last paragraphs, the first sentence of a
section, or a question they ask and then answer. But a claim can also be left for the reader to
draw from a story or a comparison: then write it in your own words, as the author would say it,
and mark it `shown`, with the anchors of the passage that makes it.

Test: say the chapter's thesis in one sentence, then say how each other claim serves it (it
supports, explains, limits or defends it). A claim that serves nothing in this chapter is a side
note, or the start of a later chapter's point.

### Concepts

| Kind | What it looks like in the text | Nexus |
|---|---|---|
| **Defined concept** | A term the author names and explains: "I call this…", "by X I mean…", a word in italics followed by a definition | The alignment problem |
| **Typology** | A set of kinds: "three levels", "two types", "first… second… third…" | Objective, subjective and intersubjective reality |
| **Contrast** | Two things set against each other | Information as representation versus information as connection |
| **Views or uses** | Rival ways to see or use the same thing, often each with a name | The naive and the populist views of information |
| **Mechanism** | A process that does something: its steps and what drives them | A self-correcting mechanism that finds and fixes errors |
| **Dilemma** | Two goals that pull apart, so more of one costs some of the other | Truth and order |
| **Indirect distinction** | A contrast the author builds across a chapter or the whole book without one sentence that names it: paired examples, the same two terms set against each other again and again | Distributed versus centralised networks, shown through democracy and dictatorship in many chapters |
| **Fiction: the story's own concepts** | Invented words, ranks, rules of a world, factions, a magic system, social codes the plot depends on | (Pride and Prejudice: the entail that passes Longbourn to Mr Collins) |

Leave out ordinary words used in their ordinary sense, and concepts the author only mentions in
passing. When unsure whether the author defines something or only uses it, check whether the
book explains it or builds on it later: if yes, it counts.

**Indirect distinctions** need the most care. Read for two terms that keep coming back as a pair,
examples always given in twos (Chernobyl and Three Mile Island), a chapter built as "the old way,
then the new way", a conclusion that only makes sense if two things differ. Record the
distinction in the author's own terms; never name it with a term the book does not use or imply.

### Examples

Every case, story, person, number or comparison the author gives. An example always serves a
claim or a concept: an example without its point is trivia, so never record one alone. Find what
it serves, and its job, from what it does where it stands, not from "for example":

- **The removal test.** If this example were gone, which claim would be less clear or less
  believable? That is the claim it serves.
- **Its job** is what it does for that claim:

| Job | What it does | Nexus |
|---|---|---|
| illustration | makes a concept concrete | Cher Ami, the war pigeon, as army branding: a brand |
| evidence | makes a claim believable | The bitcoin pizzas: the price changed, the calories did not, so price is intersubjective |
| counter-example | shows that a view fails | Chimpanzees have the same material interests and form no nations, so the Marxist view fails |
| analogy | explains one thing by its likeness to another | record which part matches which, and where the author says the likeness stops |
| running case | a case the book uses in more than one chapter | record its job in each chapter: an illustration first can be evidence later |

- **A point the author never states.** The example's place makes its point: write the point in
  your own words, mark the link `shown`, and anchor both the example and the claim it serves.
- **A long case** (a history, a trial, a disaster over several pages) is a small argument of its
  own: record its chain of causes as relations, and the claim it serves.

### Weight

For each claim, concept and example, note how many paragraphs it takes and whether later chapters
use it. A concept that later chapters build on is **core**. Weight, not kind, decides how much room
an item gets in the guide: a diagram of its own, a branch of the mind map, a leaf, or a phrase in
the Recap.

## Relations

A relation is two items and the words that link them, as a sentence the author could write:
"Stories create intersubjective reality". A concept map is built of such sentences. There are two
levels, and a chapter needs both:

- **The argument**: how passages serve the chapter's claims (this story supports that claim; this
  paragraph answers an objection). The outline records it.
- **The content**: how the concepts relate in the author's account of the world (stories create
  intersubjective reality; order often clashes with truth). The Relations section records it.

### What each passage does (the outline)

Go through the chapter passage by passage (a paragraph, or a few that make one point). For each,
write one line: its point in your own words, and its job:

| Job | The passage… |
|---|---|
| set up | asks the question or names the problem that the next passages answer |
| claim | states a point the chapter argues |
| support | gives evidence or a reason for a claim, before or after it |
| explain | shows how or why a claim holds: the mechanism |
| illustrate | makes a concept concrete |
| contrast | sets a case or a view against another |
| limit | says where a claim stops holding, or grants part of another view |
| object or answer | raises a view against a claim, or replies to one |
| consequence | draws what follows from a claim |
| generalise or apply | moves from cases to a rule, or from a rule to a new case |
| link back | uses an earlier chapter's concept: applies, extends or revises it, or answers a question left open |

The job comes from what the passage means and where it stands. A story right after a claim
usually supports or illustrates it; two cases told one after the other usually ask to be compared;
a passage that opens with what others believe usually sets up an objection or a contrast. Each job
except "set up" and "claim" links this passage to another: name that other passage's claim in the
line.

### How authors link ideas without saying so

- **Side by side.** Two cases told together ask to be compared. Ask why the author puts them
  together: what differs between them, and what the difference explains.
- **A story as an argument.** A history told in order. Order alone is not a cause: record a cause
  only where the author treats the earlier event as a reason for the later one (the story is built
  so that the outcome needs it, or the author says so later).
- **The point after the story, or never.** An anecdote opens the chapter and its claim comes pages
  later, or never comes. Join them with the removal test; when the point is never stated, mark the
  relation `shown`.
- **The shape of the chapter.** Old way, then new way; a problem, the answers that fail, then the
  answer; a claim, an objection, then a reply. The shape is itself a relation: the new way replaces
  the old; the reply defeats the objection.
- **A new name for an old thing.** When the author describes an earlier example again with a new
  concept, the new concept explains the old example.
- **Conditions.** "In large networks", "without a free press": the claim holds only under a
  condition. Record the condition: the claim needs it.
- **The author's stance.** It shows in irony, in what the author calls naive or dangerous, in which
  side gets more room and the last word, not only in "I argue". Record it: the book rejects X
  because of Y.
- **Whose view.** When the author reports a view, its relations belong to that view: "material
  interests produce stories" is the Marxist view's link, which the book rejects. Mark the view, so
  a diagram never shows its link as the author's own.
- **An unstated step.** The conclusion needs a step the author does not write. Record it only when
  the book's own words elsewhere support it; otherwise leave the gap.
- **Across chapters.** A concept from an earlier chapter comes back: the chapter applies, extends
  or revises it, or answers a question that chapter left open.

### Questions that find a relation

For two items in one passage, in neighbouring passages, or one here and one in an earlier chapter,
ask the questions below. Ask them of the author's account, never of the world: what the book says
or shows, not what you know.

| Ask | If yes | Link words (examples) |
|---|---|---|
| In the author's account, if A changed, would B change? Which way? | A causes, raises or lowers B | create, lead to, weaken |
| Does B need A? Can B exist without it? | A enables B | need, depend on, allow |
| Does A stop or hold back B? | A limits B | stop at, block |
| Is A a kind, a case or a part of B? | A belongs to B | kind of, part of |
| Does the author set A against B, or show that more of one means less of the other? | A opposes B, or they trade off | clash with, cost |
| Does A come back round, through B, to strengthen or weaken itself? | a loop | feed, in turn |
| Does A find or fix B's errors? | A corrects B | expose, check |
| Does the author show A turning into B, or replacing it? | A becomes or replaces B | replace, become |
| What is A for? Which problem does it solve? | A serves B | solve, serve |
| Would a claim fall or blur without A? | A supports the claim | show, support |

### Check each relation before you record it

Write the relation as a sentence the author could have written. Then read its passage again and
ask: would the author agree with this sentence? Would a careful reader of these paragraphs alone
see it? Mark it:

- `stated`: one sentence of the book says it.
- `shown`: a passage shows it without saying it (a story and its point, two cases side by side,
  the shape of a section).
- `joined`: it joins two or more passages (A leads to B on one page, B to C on another). Give
  every anchor.
- Possible but not shown, or it needs knowledge from outside the book: leave it out.

In fiction the same reading finds why people act and what causes what. A feeling, motive or piece
of knowledge goes in only where the text gives the reader clear grounds for it: many false
statements in AI summaries of novels are of this kind (`references/verify.md`).

## Record it (step 5)

The fact sheet of a non-fiction chapter has these sections (the anchors are examples):

```
## Outline (each passage: point · job)
- 011 p1–p6 · set up · how did Sapiens come to rule the world?
- 011 p7–p12 · claim C1 · personal bonds stop at a few hundred people
- 011 p13–p18 · support C2 · Stalin's cult: followers connect to a story, not to the man
## Claims
- C0 thesis · Sapiens rule because stories let them cooperate in unlimited numbers · 011 p1
- C1 · personal bonds stop at a few hundred people · 011 p8
- C2 · strangers cooperate through human-to-story chains · 011 p12
## Concepts and distinctions
- {Three kinds of reality} typology · stated · objective (exists whatever people think: stones) /
  subjective (in one mind: pain) / intersubjective (between many minds: money, nations) · 011 p22–p25
- {Truth and order} dilemma · stated · every network must both find truth and keep order;
  order is often easier with fiction · 011 p59–p62
## Examples
- {The bitcoin pizzas} evidence · for {Intersubjective reality} · the price changed, the calories did not · 011 p27
- {Chimpanzees} counter-example · against {Marxist view} · the same interests, no nations · 011 p40
## Relations
- {Personal bonds} · limit · {Cooperation} · stated · 011 p8
- {Human-to-story chains} · lift the limit of · {Personal bonds} · joined · 011 p8, 011 p12
- {Material interests} · produce · {Stories} · stated · view: Marxist, rejected · 011 p38
- ← ch1 {Information as connection} · applied to · {Stories} · shown · 011 p3
```

- Names in braces are inventory names: the same name for the same thing on every line and in every
  chapter.
- A relation line: `{from}` · link words (≤ 4, the book's own where it has them) · `{to}` ·
  `stated`, `shown` or `joined` · anchors. A reported view adds `view: <name>, rejected` (or
  `accepted`).
- A link to an earlier chapter names that chapter and the concept (`← ch1 {Information as
  connection}`), with an anchor in this chapter only: the checker accepts only anchors in the
  chapter's own text files.
- An indirect distinction is a concept line marked `shown` (or `joined`), with the anchors of the
  passages that build it.

Then, in the plan (step 4, kept as a list in `work/<id>/concepts.md`), gather every concept from
every fact sheet into one **concept inventory**, in book order, with the chapter that introduces
each and its weight. A **core** concept always appears in a diagram of the chapter that introduces
it, and later chapters may name it again.

## Show it (step 6)

The diagrams come from the relations: `references/visuals.md`, "From relations to diagrams". Then:

- **Every concept and distinction is shown** in the chapter that introduces it: in a diagram, its
  own or the one of the group of relations it belongs to. A minor item, met in one or two
  paragraphs and never built on, needs only a mind map leaf, a key point or (Story and Reading
  guides) its row in the PDF-only `list` "Terms" (name in the book's words, a definition ≤ 18
  words).
- **Examples go with their claim**, with their job clear: evidence under the claim it supports, a
  counter-example next to the view it refutes.
- **A reported view's links** are drawn as that view's (the `negative` side, the rejected option),
  never as the author's.
- **Points and terms** may name a concept again, but a concept that appears only in text has not
  been shown.
- **No key concepts chapter.** The guide has only the book's own chapters
  (`references/guides.md`), so each concept lives in the chapter that introduces it.
- **Stable cues.** A core concept keeps its inventory name, in the book's words, on every screen
  of every chapter and style; a later chart that shows it again uses the same chart kind and the
  same labels for its members (the three kinds of reality are always "Objective", "Subjective" and
  "Intersubjective", in the same order). A cue the reader learned must work again.
- With a limit (`upTo`): only concepts introduced up to the limit.

## Check it (step 7)

Go through the inventory line by line and find each item on a screen of the chapter that
introduces it: in a diagram (minor items: a leaf, a key point or a Terms row). Then check that
every link drawn in a diagram (an arrow, a link label, a leaf under its branch, a row on a side) is
a line in the fact sheet's Relations or Examples; the fresh-eyes review checks those lines against
their passages (`references/verify.md`). Last, read the chapter's text once more for what you did
not record: a pair of terms set against each other more than once is the most common miss, and a
passage whose job you cannot say is the next.

## Basis

- Concept maps are built of propositions, two concepts and the words that link them (Novak and
  Gowin, *Learning How to Learn*, 1984): a diagram without links is a list.
- The parts of a text are linked by relations such as evidence, cause, contrast and concession,
  which readers find from meaning, often with no linking word (Mann and Thompson, rhetorical
  structure theory, 1988).
